Animal movement

When, where, and why do animals move?

Using acoustic telemetry, accelerometers, and long-term tracking, I follow individual mesopredators through the coastal Everglades to learn how temperature, water levels, and body size shape what they do.

  • Common Snook activity dropped sharply above 26 °C, and fish switched between diurnal and nocturnal activity at temperature extremes. White et al. 2026, Royal Society Open Science
  • In wetter years, snook showed greater individual specialization in space use, and, contrary to predictions, their trophic niche contracted. Santos et al. 2025, Scientific Reports
  • Most snook tagged in the Shark River emigrated, some traveling up to ~88 km, indicating that snook use large portions of the seascape. Goldner et al. 2026, Marine and Coastal Fisheries
  • Spotted Seatrout habitat selection integrates information on patch- and seascape-scale characteristics in Florida Bay. Rodemann et al. 2025, Scientific Reports

Nutrients and food webs

How do animals move nutrients and energy through ecosystems?

Fish recycle elements within and across seemingly disparate ecosystems. I measure those nutrient and energy fluxes, from a single spawning run to decades of data across vastly different ocean ecosystems.

  • An estimated 67,000 migrating Smallmouth Buffalo delivered ~730 kg of nitrogen and ~80 kg of phosphorus to Citico Creek in a single spawning season. White et al. 2023, Ecology of Freshwater Fish
  • Migrating redhorses delivered a spring pulse of nitrogen, mostly through their eggs, when the stream was exporting little nitrogen of its own. Hudson et al. 2024, Ecology of Freshwater Fish
  • Species richness had strong, positive effects on the stability of consumer-mediated nutrient dynamics (CND) across ecosystems, though species asynchrony was the primary driver within ecosystems. White et al. 2026, PNAS
  • In dry, warm years, snook faced a mismatch: energy demands rose while diet quality fell, showing that climate change can limit consumers from both sides of the equation. White et al. 2025, Marine and Coastal Fisheries

Rivers under global change

How do flow loss, warming, and invasions reshape river communities?

Rivers in the Colorado River basin are drying as demand for water grows and temperatures warm, and Ohio rivers face invasive carp and changing land use. I study what these pressures mean for native fishes and aquatic insects.

  • Across seven Colorado River basin rivers, aquatic insect communities were less diverse where flows have declined the most. White et al., in review, Ecohydrology
  • In a dryland river long considered a refuge, native fish densities collapsed from 2008 to 2025 while nonnatives remained abundant. Porter et al., in review, Transactions of the American Fisheries Society
